The Delaware structure: what it does and does not solve for an Argentine company

Founders are told the flip is the answer. It resolves one set of problems precisely, and creates another that is discovered, reliably, in diligence — two years later, when it is expensive.

By the Cross-Border Brief desk 27 August 2026 11 min Stated as at 27 Aug 2026 Originally published in Spanish

Executive summary

Almost every Argentine company that raises institutional capital in the United States ends up beneath a Delaware holding company. The structure is well understood and, for its purpose, correct. What it does not do is make the Argentine operating company disappear — and the assumptions founders carry into the flip about intellectual property, corporate history, tax and employment are where financings slow down or fail.

Key takeaways

  1. The flip solves investor-side problems: familiar governing law, standard documents, a recognisable exit path. Those are real and worth having.
  2. It does not solve operating-side problems. The Argentine entity continues to exist, employ people, own contracts and generate obligations.
  3. Most diligence failures are not new problems. They are two-year-old problems that were inexpensive to fix at the time and are not inexpensive now.
  4. Intellectual property is the most common single point of failure — not because assignment is difficult, but because it is assumed to have happened.
  5. The structure has tax consequences in both jurisdictions that must be modelled together. Optimising one side in isolation is how the other side's exposure is created.

What the structure is for

The pattern is consistent enough to be near-universal. An Argentine company reaches the point of raising institutional capital, and its prospective investors — almost always American — require a US holding company, typically incorporated in Delaware, sitting above the Argentine operating entity, with the founders' and existing shareholders' interests exchanged for equity at the top.

This is not arbitrary. Institutional venture and growth investors deploy from funds with their own constraints, invest on documents their counsel has seen a thousand times, and price an exit against precedents that all sit in the same jurisdiction. Presenting them with an Argentine sociedad anónima and a request to adapt is not a negotiation — it is, in practice, a decline.

The flip is not a tax structure or a clever arrangement. It is a translation of a company into a form the capital can read.

Understood that way, its merits are clear and its limits are equally clear. A translation does not change the thing translated. The Argentine company is still an Argentine company, with Argentine employees, Argentine contracts, Argentine tax obligations and an Argentine corporate history that a US buyer will eventually read.

Where it stops working

Intellectual property

The most frequent failure is also the most avoidable. Investors need the holding company to own the intellectual property the business runs on. Founders generally assume it does, because the company paid for it and the people who built it were on the payroll.

That assumption depends on facts: who created what, under what contractual arrangement, whether assignment language existed and was effective, and whether the relevant transfers were actually documented at the time rather than reconstructed afterwards. Early-stage companies routinely accumulate contributions from contractors, advisers, university collaborators and founders working before incorporation — none of which is a problem when it is handled, and all of which is a problem when it is discovered in a data room. Corporate history

The second failure is quieter. Argentine corporate housekeeping — meetings, filings, registrations, capital movements, the paper trail behind share issuances — is assessed at diligence against a standard the company was not applying at the time. Gaps that a local adviser would regard as ordinary and curable can read to US counsel as unresolved title to the very shares being exchanged.

Nothing here is usually fatal. The difficulty is timing. A defect identified at Series A can be cured in weeks by people who remember the facts. The same defect identified at Series C, with a signed term sheet and a closing date, is a repricing conversation.

Tax on both sides

A flip is a transaction, and transactions have tax consequences for the shareholders exchanging their interests and for the entities involved — in Argentina, in the United States, and in the interaction between them. Structures optimised for one jurisdiction in isolation are the reliable source of the other jurisdiction's problem, and the modelling has to be done jointly, before the exchange rather than after. People

After the flip, the group typically has a US holding company with no employees and an Argentine company with all of them. That arrangement raises questions about how the operating entity is funded, how services between the entities are priced, whether the arrangement creates any presence for the holding company in Argentina, and how equity granted at the top is treated for people employed at the bottom. Each is answerable. None answers itself. [TRANSFER PRICING, PERMANENT ESTABLISHMENT AND EQUITY TREATMENT — VERIFY]

Argentina

Argentina implications

The operating company remains fully subject to Argentine law and continues to carry the obligations it had before the structure existed. Practically, the questions that matter are whether the historical corporate record supports the shares being exchanged, whether intellectual property was effectively assigned as it was created, and what the exchange itself triggers locally. These are diligence questions long before they are structuring questions, which is why they are best answered while they are still cheap. [VERIFY WITH ARGENTINE-ADMITTED COUNSEL]

United States

US and New York implications

The holding company will be governed by Delaware corporate law and financed on documents drafted under New York law. The representations the company gives in those documents are the mechanism by which every unresolved Argentine question becomes a US contractual exposure — which is the real reason diligence is unforgiving. A representation about title, or about ownership of intellectual property, does not become less true because the underlying facts are in another jurisdiction and another language. [VERIFY WITH NEW YORK-ADMITTED COUNSEL]

What management should consider

  1. Do the intellectual property audit before you need it. Establish who created what, under what arrangement, and whether assignment was documented at the time. This is the single highest-return piece of preparatory work available to an Argentine company that intends to raise in the United States.
  2. Reconstruct the corporate record while the facts are recoverable. The people who attended the meeting and signed the document are still reachable now. That will be less true at Series C.
  3. Model the tax position in both jurisdictions together, before the exchange. Get advice that covers both sides in one exercise rather than two opinions that do not refer to each other.
  4. Decide the intercompany arrangement deliberately. How the Argentine entity is funded and how services are priced should be a documented decision, not an accumulation of practice.
  5. Treat the flip as a deadline, not an event. Everything above is cheaper before it than after, and none of it becomes easier by waiting.
