What this is

Entering a market is a sequencing problem before it is a legal one. The questions that determine whether an entry succeeds are usually commercial and practical: what has to exist before anything else can happen, how long each step actually takes, what it costs to hold the position while waiting, and which decisions are difficult to reverse once taken.

We map that sequence against the specific market, sector and timetable — and we tell you where the estimates are soft. An entry plan that presents every timeline as certain is not a plan; it is a proposal.

What an engagement produces

  • Entry sequenceThe ordered set of steps, with dependencies made explicit — what must precede what, and which steps can run in parallel.
  • Timetable with confidence bandsRealistic durations, stated as ranges rather than dates, with the assumptions behind each one visible and challengeable.
  • Cost modelEstablishment and carrying costs across the sequence, including the cost of waiting — which is usually the number that has not been calculated.
  • Regulatory mapWhich authorisations, registrations and regimes apply, who administers them, and which are gating rather than parallel. Legal conclusions are referred, not asserted.
  • Decision pointsThe specific moments where a choice becomes hard to reverse, so they are taken deliberately rather than by default.
  • Reversal analysisWhat would have to change for the entry thesis to fail, and which of those changes are observable in advance.

How this works with the legal side

Most entries need both. The advisory engagement establishes the sequence and the economics; the legal engagement executes the steps that require admitted counsel. They are separate engagements, separately scoped and separately billed, and either can be taken without the other.

We will tell you which you need. If the honest answer is that you only need one, that is the answer you will get.
