Argentina × New York

The corridor is not a proposition. It is the existing architecture.

Argentine cross-border business already runs on New York law. What is missing is not a bridge — it is counsel that understands both ends of one that already carries the traffic.

Argentine sovereign and corporate debt has for decades been issued under New York law, with submission to the courts of the Southern District of New York. Cross-border financings are documented under it. The restructuring litigation that followed Argentina's 2001 default was argued in Manhattan, not Buenos Aires. Argentina is a party to the New York Convention, so arbitral awards involving Argentine parties are enforceable across the relationship. And Argentine founders who raise institutional venture capital do so, almost without exception, through a Delaware holding company drafted by New York lawyers.

None of this is accidental, and none of it is a preference. New York law governs because it is what makes Argentine paper financeable: a body of contract law with deep precedent, a court that has actually decided the hard questions, and remedies an international lender can price. A borrower who insists on local law is not asserting sovereignty. They are asking for a discount.

The legal operating system of Argentine cross-border business is already substantially American. The question is not whether to bridge two markets. It is who is competent at the seam between them.

What this produces is a permanent structural asymmetry. An Argentine business operates under Argentine law — its employees, its contracts, its tax, its regulator, its corporate record. It finances under New York law, and it will one day be bought or listed under it too. One company, two legal systems, and a single transaction that has to satisfy both.

Almost every serious failure in cross-border work happens at that junction. Not in Buenos Aires, where local counsel is generally excellent. Not in New York, where the documents are drafted by people who have done it a thousand times. At the translation between them — which is nobody's file.

Where it actually fails

Five failures, in roughly descending order of how often we see them. Each is a story a prospective client has already lived.

  1. Enforcement mechanics that do not survive contact with Argentine procedureAn agreement drafted in New York can contain remedies, notice provisions and security arrangements that are elegant on the page and unavailable in practice. The document is not wrong. It was simply never tested against the procedure that would have to give effect to it.
  2. Diligence that answers the wrong question correctlyAn Argentine due diligence report can be thorough, accurate and entirely unhelpful — because it answers Argentine questions in Argentine terms and never addresses what a US investment committee needs in order to approve the transaction. Both sides then wait for the other to translate.
  3. Tax efficiency on one side creating exposure on the otherA structure optimised for United States treatment produces an Argentine consequence that nobody modelled, or the reverse. Discovered after signing, it is a repricing. Discovered before, it is a drafting note.
  4. Regulatory change arriving late and stripped of its meaningSomething moves in Buenos Aires. It reaches the New York deal team weeks later, in translation, summarised by someone who understood the text but not the commercial implication. By then the window it opened or closed has moved.
  5. Corporate history that makes a company unfinanceableIntellectual property never formally assigned. Share issuances with an incomplete paper trail. Each was cheap to fix when it happened and expensive to fix in a data room with a signed term sheet and a closing date.

Why this is hard to buy

The corridor is well served at each end and poorly served in the middle, and the reason is structural rather than a failure of competence.

A New York firm can draft anything, but it cannot assess Argentine regulatory risk from first principles — so it prices what it cannot assess, usually conservatively, and passes the cost to the client. An Argentine firm knows exactly what the regulation means, but is not running the process to the standard a US investment committee applies, because that is not the market it serves. Both are doing their jobs. Neither engagement covers the join, and the client discovers this at the worst possible moment.

The alternative that most groups fall into — retaining both and coordinating them internally — puts the translation risk on the client's own general counsel, who is the person least able to carry it and most likely to be blamed for it.

We are organised around the join. That is the entire proposition, and it is why the firm exists in this shape rather than as a larger practice in either city.
